When a miner finds a block, every other miner on the network is suddenly working on something that no longer matters. Their current job builds on a tip that just became stale, and until they switch, any hash they find is most likely going to lose.

So how long does that switch take? And what happens in between?

I spent a couple of days measuring this on my own node, and looking at what miners on mainnet seem to be doing about it. This is what I found.

What happens when a new block arrives?

Most nodes today learn about a new block through compact block relay (BIP 152). Instead of sending the whole block, a peer sends the header plus short ids for the transactions in it. The idea is that we probably already have most of those transactions in our mempool, so we can rebuild the block locally.

From the moment we see the header to the moment the block becomes our new tip, there are roughly four steps:

  • fetch: getting the compact block itself, if the peer only announced it.
  • fill: matching the short ids against our mempool.
  • round trip: if some transactions are missing, asking the peer for them (getblocktxn) and waiting for the answer (blocktxn).
  • connect: validating the block and making it our tip.

Fill and connect are local work. Fetch and the round trip are network, and depend on how far away (in time) our peers are. That distance is the round trip time, or RTT, basically your ping.

A miner getting templates from this node can only start on a full template for the new block after the last step. Everything before that is time spent hashing on the old tip.

What did I measure?

I turned on logtimemicros and debug=cmpctblock on my mainnet node and wrote a script that reads debug.log and splits every block into the four steps above. I left it running over 129 blocks (976408 to 976536).

To be upfront about the setup, this node is a worst case. It is on 4G behind carrier grade NAT, so it can only make outbound connections, and its ping to peers was around 200 to 450 ms.

Here is what it looked like, leaving out 12 blocks where my link stalled and 4 blocks right after a restart:


stage                         median     p90      max
fetch after header  (network)  151 ms   556 ms   1.5 s
round trip          (network)  276 ms   1.8 s    8.6 s
fill from mempool   (local)      8 ms    10 ms    13 ms
connect / validate  (local)     63 ms   107 ms   395 ms
header -> tip total            512 ms   1.8 s    10.3 s

A few things stood out:

  • 75% of blocks (85 of 113) needed a round trip, so rebuilding the block from the mempool alone was the minority case.
  • The local work was only around 70 ms. Almost all of the time was spent waiting on the network.
  • The 12 stalled blocks took anywhere from 2.8 to 50 seconds. That is about 1 in 10 blocks.

So on a link like mine, the gap is usually under a second, but multi-second gaps are not rare.

But isn't that just a bad connection?

Partly, yes. If I model the same blocks as local time plus the number of round trips times the RTT, a node with a 50 ms RTT would see around 160 ms median and 500 ms at worst.

But two things make it more than a bad connection.

First, the round trips. Over half of them (46 of 85) were for just 1 to 5 missing transactions. That is not my link, that is my mempool not matching the miner's. And it is not just me: 0xB10C, who monitors this across the network, reported that from mid July 2026 blocks that could be rebuilt without a round trip were hovering between 1% and 5%. That lasted until the unsolicited transaction chains being pushed to nodes stopped on 2026-10-18.

Second, links like mine are exactly what a lot of small miners have. Home miners, small pools, and miners building their own templates with Stratum v2 are more likely to be on a slow or unreliable connection than a large pool with servers next to each other.

At 1 to 3 seconds per block, that is roughly 0.2% to 0.5% of their hashrate spent on a stale tip, since a block comes about every 600 seconds.

What are miners doing about it today?

To get a picture of this, I looked at the last year of blocks (923849 to 976408). There were 97 empty blocks, blocks with only the coinbase transaction in them. For each block, I compared its header timestamp with its parent's:


gap to parent   empty blocks     all blocks
0-10s           20% (19/97)      1.1%
0-30s           58% (56/97)      4.1%
0-60s           76% (74/97)      8.1%

Empty blocks are roughly 9 to 17 times more likely than other blocks to come right after their parent.

Why would that be? There are two common explanations:

  • Mining on the header (sometimes called spy mining). The pool starts on the new block as soon as it sees the header, before downloading or validating it. Since it does not know yet which transactions the parent included, the only safe thing to mine is an empty block.
  • An empty job while the full template is being built. Even after validating the block, building a new full template takes some time, so some pools send an empty job first and replace it shortly after.

The data cannot tell these apart, and header timestamps are noisy since miners set them. Blocks with a timestamp earlier than their parent are left out of the table for that reason. But either way the takeaway is the same: miners already prefer to start right away on an empty block over waiting for a full one.

So what could Bitcoin Core do?

This is what bitcoin/bitcoin#33756 is about. Bitcoin Core's mining interface has a waitNext() call that gives a client like the Stratum v2 Template Provider a new template when something changes. The idea in the issue is to let it optionally return an empty template as soon as we see a valid header for a new block, before the block is downloaded and validated.

A small first version of that could look like this:

  • Opt in only, off by default.
  • Only an empty template, and only for a header that directly extends our validated tip.
  • Only if the block has not connected within a short delay, so well connected nodes would rarely produce one.
  • Replaced by a full template once the block connects, or by a template on the old tip if the block turns out invalid or never shows up.

There are trade-offs. Mining on a header you have not validated means that, if the block turns out invalid, you briefly mined on top of something worthless. An invalid header still needs a full block's proof of work, so this is expensive to abuse, but it is still a risk the miner should choose to take. There is also the question of fees: an empty block gives up the fees. During the gap though, the alternative is mining on a tip that is already stale, and you cannot build a full block on the new tip until you have the parent's transactions anyway.

This also is not competing with work that makes compact block relay itself better, like prefilling compact blocks. Prefill should fix most of the small 1 to 5 transaction misses. What is left are the big misses (one block in my data needed 6556 transactions, 607 kB, fetched after the header) and link stalls, which prefill cannot cover.
